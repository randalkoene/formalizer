What this app is

      
A diagnostic view of your unread Gmail. It sorts unread mail into
      categories and shows, for every message, exactly why it landed where it did.

      
Nothing here modifies your mailbox. The OAuth token is scoped
      gmail.readonly, so Google refuses any change regardless of what this code
      asks for. Reading a message here does not mark it read in Gmail.

      
It works on a local cache. Fetching copies mail into
      cache/; everything else — scoring, filtering, teaching — runs against that
      copy, offline and instantly.

    


    

      
Starting and stopping

      
The server is an ordinary program. Started in a terminal, Ctrl+C
      stops it. Started in the background, Ctrl+C in some other terminal
      does nothing at all — the old one keeps the port, the browser keeps showing a page, and
      a new one refuses to start.

      

        
          
./serve.pystart it

          
./serve.py --statusis one running, and as which pid

          
./serve.py --stopstop whichever one holds the port

          
./serve.py --port 8712run a second one alongside

        
      

      
Trying to start one while another holds the port now prints the
      offending pid and its command line, with these commands — rather than an
      "address already in use" traceback.

      
Stopping the server loses nothing. Everything lives in cache/,
      config.yaml and corrections.yaml on disk. Write mode is the one
      thing that does not survive: a fresh server always starts read-only.

      
So if you restart the server with a browser tab already open, that
      tab is talking to a read-only server again. The badge re-checks with the server every ten
      seconds and corrects itself, and any action refused in the meantime says plainly that
      write mode is off rather than failing quietly.

Why Sync exists

      
This app learns what happened to a message only by asking Gmail. When you reply in
      Gmail, read something on your phone, or archive from another client, that message stops
      being unread-in-inbox — but the local cache has no way to notice.

      
Sync asks once: it lists what is unread right now, drops everything in
      the cache that no longer is, downloads only genuinely new messages, and re-scores. It
      takes seconds because it never re-downloads what it already has. A full rebuild re-fetches
      all two thousand messages and takes minutes.

      
Replying, one message at a time

      
Pressing Reply in Gmail opens Gmail in a new tab and puts a small
      prompt here naming that message. Reply there, come back, and press Replied /
      read — remove it: the app checks that one message against Gmail and drops it
      from triage, in about a second. Cancel leaves it exactly where it was.

      
The prompt does not take your word for it. It asks Gmail whether the message is still
      unread in your inbox, and if it somehow is — you closed the tab without opening it, say —
      it tells you and changes nothing rather than quietly losing the message.

      
This exists so you do not have to reply to five things and then try to remember which
      five. Settle each one as you go. Sync in the top bar is still there for
      everything else: mail you read on your phone, archived elsewhere, or that simply arrived.


      
What you have taught the classifier lives in
      corrections.yaml and is never touched by any of the three.


      
Reading the progress panel

      
A run moves through three states, and the panel always says which one it is in:

      
    
        
  • Fetching — the bar tracks the current phase: messages, then threads, then
            senders. The phase name and counts sit beside it.
    • 
        
  • Scoring… — the bar sweeps instead of filling, because there is no countable
            unit here. Takes a couple of seconds. The fetch is already finished at this point.
    • 
        
  • Done — the panel turns green, the bar reads complete, and a summary
            line states what landed: messages, threads, senders, and how many need your
            attention.
    • 
      

      
If the bar stops short of 100%, that is not a stall — it means some items genuinely
      could not be fetched, and the warning box above the log says how many and why. Everything
      else is still cached and scored.

      
Scoring runs automatically after a fetch, so there is nothing to
      press afterwards. The log's last line is always Re-scored N messages. when
      a run has fully completed.

    


    

      
The panels


      
Two kinds of attention

      
Every message is judged on two separate questions. What kind of mail is this?
      — that is the category. And what does it want from you? — that is the
      needs axis, shown as a pill on each row:

      
    
        
  • reply — someone is waiting on an answer. A question
            aimed at you, a request, a deadline, or a direct reply to something you sent.
    • 
        
  • read — worth a glance, but nothing is being asked:
            grant and org business, threads you are in, a list you follow, a booking
            confirmation, a security alert.
    • 
        
  • no pill — nothing needs you.
    • 
      

      
The two are deliberately independent. A grant message that asks you to confirm a budget
      is filed under Grants & Funding but still says reply; a mailing list
      you follow stays Bulk but says read. Neither axis has to lie to keep the
      other honest.


      
Triage — the left rail

      
The two numbers at the top are the ones that matter: how much is waiting on a
      reply, and how much is worth reading. Click either to
      see just that. The thin striped bar underneath is the whole inbox by category, to scale —
      the grey majority is bulk and automated mail.

      
Below that the rail has three groups. Needs you filters by the needs axis.
      By kind filters by category. Worth a look inside the noise filters by tag —
      the handful of things inside Bulk and Automated that usually deserve a glance: lists you
      follow, travel bookings, invoices, security alerts, calendar invites.


      
Old mail

      
Unread mail gains weight for about a month — it has been sitting there — and then
      loses it again. By four months the age bonus is gone; by a year it is a penalty. Something
      unanswered that long has usually been overtaken by events.

      
But "usually" is not "always", so nothing is hidden. Every list splits at
      120 days: recent mail first, then a collapsed Older than 120 days
      band with its count. Open it when you want to sweep the backlog; ignore it the rest of the
      time. The score breakdown always says which way age pushed a message, and by how much.


      
Triage — the message list

      
Search matches sender, subject and snippet; multiple words must all match. Sort by
      score, date or sender. Each row shows its score, who sent it, the top reasons that
      produced that score, and its age. Small pills mark thread length, whether you have
      replied in that thread, and attachments. The coloured stripe on the left is the
      category.


      
Read-only and write mode

      
The badge at the top left is a switch. read-only is where the app
      starts, every time the server starts, and it is not a promise — it is enforced. The write
      credential lives in a separate file that is only loaded when you arm write mode, so in
      read-only mode the process holds nothing capable of changing your mailbox.

      
Clicking it arms write mode for 15 minutes, counting down on the
      badge, after which it disarms itself. Granting the write scope is a one-time step:
      ./triage.py authorize-write.

      
The scope requested is gmail.modify. It can mark read, archive, star,
      trash and report spam. It cannot delete permanently — Google reserves
      that for a far broader scope this tool never asks for — so everything here is reversible
      from Gmail, and trashed mail sits in Trash for about 30 days.


      
What the actions are

      
    
        
  • Reply in Gmail — always available, no write access needed. Replying happens
            in Gmail so you have everything Gmail gives you. Note that opening it there is what
            marks it read; this app never does that behind your back.
    • 
        
  • Mark read — clears the unread flag, leaves it in the inbox.
    • 
        
  • Archive — removes it from the inbox; still in All Mail.
    • 
        
  • Star — flags it in Gmail and leaves it here, still unread.
    • 
        
  • Trash — Gmail's Trash, recoverable for about 30 days.
    • 
        
  • Report spam — moves it to Spam. Offered only in the Spam category.
    • 
        
  • Unsubscribe — opens the sender's own unsubscribe link. Changes nothing in Gmail.
    • 
      

      
Which actions appear depends on where you are. Categories where every message matters
      get star, mark read, archive — no trash.
      Bulk and Automated add trash.
      Spam offers trash and report spam.
      Onboarding has no trash on purpose — applications and resumes
      are worth keeping, so that pile is read and filed, never cleared.

      
Every view with actions also has checkboxes, so you can tick several and act on them
      together — including Needs Reply, Grants, Directed, Threads and Onboarding, where plenty of messages
      only need a glance before being cleared. Batch does not widen what is possible: trash is
      still absent from those four, in bulk exactly as it is singly. Select all
      shown is in the bar above the list.

      
All messages offers no actions at all, and so has no checkboxes. That is deliberate: deciding
      what to do with something should follow from having looked at a category. The server
      enforces this too, not just the buttons — an action aimed at the catch-all view is
      refused outright.

      
Every action is written to cache/actions.log before it is sent,
      so there is a record even if the call later fails. Data → What has been done to
      your mailbox shows it, as does ./triage.py actions.


      
Triage — the detail pane

      
Why this score is the important part: every rule that fired, what it
      contributed, and in plain words why. Bars run right for points gained, left for points
      lost. If a message is in the wrong place, this tells you which rule to blame.

      
Teach the classifier — see above. Actions act on
      Gmail, and are greyed out until write mode is armed. Signals are the
      internal flags, highlighted when true.


      
Dashboard

      
Shape of the backlog: composition, age, score distribution, arrivals by month, loudest
      senders, and which scoring rules fire most (the ones worth tuning first). Bars are
      clickable — they jump into the filtered list.


      
Weights

      
Every weight and threshold with its default; anything you have overridden is
      highlighted. Read-only here: edit config.yaml, then press
      Re-score.


      
Data

      
The three refresh options, everything you have taught the classifier (with
      forget on each row), and your forwarding aliases.

    


    

      
Teaching it

      
When a message is filed wrongly, open it and use Teach the classifier.
      Pick what the correction applies to — this sender, everyone at this domain, or just this
      one message — then press one of:

      
    
        
  • Personal — never bulk — real human correspondence. Overrides every bulk and
            automated signal, however list-like the headers look.
    • 
        
  • Always important — treat like a VIP, always surface it.
    • 
        
  • This really is bulk — force it into Bulk.
    • 
        
  • Ignore this sender — noise you never want near the top.
    • 
      

      
The note field records why, and shows up in the score breakdown later.
      File this one message under pins a single message to a category outright,
      whatever the rules think.

      
Corrections save to corrections.yaml and re-score immediately. They change
      how the local cache is scored — they never touch your mailbox.


      
Lists and groups

      
Mail that arrives via a mailing list or a group address sits in Bulk, which is right
      for most of it and wrong for the few groups you actually belong to. Teach those once:
      open any message from the list and press I read this list, or use
      Data → Lists and groups, which ranks every list you have not ruled on by
      how much mail it sends so you can clear them in one pass.

      
A list you follow stays in Bulk — it really is list mail — but is tagged
      Lists you follow, counted under To read, and reachable from the
      rail. A list can be taught by its List-Id or by its address; both spellings
      are treated as the same list.


      
Onboarding

      
Intake mail — volunteer offers, resumes, internship applications — arrives through a
      group address and therefore looks exactly like a mailing list: List-Id,
      Precedence: list, an unsubscribe link. It is not. It gets its own category,
      Onboarding, which sits between the threads and the noise, is marked
      read rather than reply, and offers no trash so nothing you may want
      to refer back to is thrown away from here.

      
Which groups count is config, not code: onboarding_addresses in
      config.yaml, starting with onboarding@carboncopies.org. Either
      spelling works — the group address or its List-Id. Teaching still outranks
      it: muting the group, or marking a sender as bulk, puts that mail back in Bulk.


      
Muting is not hiding

      
Mute this list does not remove anything. Muted mail is filed in
      Bulk like any other list mail, sorted to the bottom, marked needs
      nothing, and tagged so you can pull it up from Muted — still filed in
      Bulk in the rail whenever you want to check what you muted.

      
This is deliberate. Every message in the cache is in exactly one category, always —
      that holds for muted lists, ignored senders and year-old mail alike. Nothing is dropped,
      nothing is invisible, and the counts always add up to the message total. When actions
      that modify Gmail arrive, they will be driven off these buckets, so a message that fell
      out of all of them would be a message silently exempt from whatever you chose to do.

      
Muting also never files anything as Spam. That bucket means failed
      authentication, impersonation or a lookalike domain — a newsletter you are tired of is
      not spam, and conflating the two would make the spam bucket useless for the one job it
      has.


      
Forwarding aliases

      
If mail reaches you at another address that forwards into this mailbox, declare it
      under Data → Your other addresses, or press That address is
      me on any message that shows it. This matters more than it sounds: until an
      alias is declared, mail sent to it counts as not addressed to you, earns no
      directedness credit, and tends to sink into Bulk — which is exactly how personal mail
      ends up looking like a newsletter.
